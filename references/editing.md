# Editing decisions

Use these decisions selectively. No individual trait proves machine authorship. The goal is a stronger piece of writing, not elimination of every feature that has appeared in an AI corpus.

## 1. Content before cosmetic substitution

An argument made of generic claims remains generic after its synonyms change. First ask what the reader learns or can do that they could not learn from a generic description of the topic.

**The substitution test:** Could the paragraph survive unchanged if the product, organization, or topic name were replaced? If yes, inspect it for missing substance. Some definitions and transitions are legitimately general; keep them if they serve the reader.

**The consequence test:** Does the detail change the conclusion, explain a constraint, or enable an action? A camera's obstructed battery compartment matters if it changes tripod use. A string of adjectives about “premium performance” tells the reader little. Only use a consequential detail if the source supplies it or new research verifies it.

**The usable specificity test:** A recommendation should identify what to do, in what situation, and why. “Strengthen collaboration” may be all the source supports; do not invent weekly meetings, owners, or targets. In an editing task, point out what information is missing. In a drafting task, use clearly marked proposals if the brief permits recommendations.

**The bounds test:** Keep population, location, time, measured outcome, comparison group, uncertainty, and exceptions attached to a claim. Replace unsupported universal praise with the defensible claim already present. Do not broaden a pilot result into a commercial guarantee.

**The cost test:** Preserve the downside that made a decision difficult. A cheaper option can remain inconvenient. A successful pilot can still leave deployment untested. A conclusion need not dissolve the trade-off into an “ideal balance.”

These content-level questions are informed by IdeaLens [R01] but their use as editing checks is an editorial application, not an experimentally validated humanizer recipe.

## 2. Paragraph function and progression

Map each paragraph to its job: establish a fact, explain a mechanism, present evidence, address an objection, describe a scene, or request an action. Merge or remove paragraphs whose only function is to repeat an earlier statement in grander words.

Common repairs:

| Symptom | Useful repair | Keep when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generic “in today's world” opening | Start at the actual event, question, or decision | Historical or social context is needed to understand the claim |
| Repeated claim–three bullets–summary template | Arrange material by the reader's task or argument | The template supports scanning, comparison, or a mandated format |
| Every paragraph starts with “Furthermore” or “Additionally” | Express the actual relation, or remove a redundant connector | A transition prevents a real logical jump |
| Every section ends by saying it is important | End on evidence, implication, action, or the next question | The sentence adds a distinct consequence |
| Forced “not X but Y” contrast | State the intended distinction directly | Both alternatives are relevant and genuinely opposed |
| Equal treatment of every side | Give space in proportion to the evidence and task | Balanced coverage is a real requirement |

Do not flatten exposition into bullet fragments by default. Lists are useful for parallel actions or alternatives; prose is usually better for developing an argument. Keep headings that help navigation, especially in long documents.

## 3. Sentence construction

Noun-heavy writing can hide the action: “implementation of an assessment” may simply mean “assess.” Recover the verb when it keeps the technical meaning. A term such as “randomization” may name a precise procedure and should remain.

Find the responsible actor before converting passive to active. “The samples were excluded” does not justify “We excluded the samples” if the source does not establish who did it. Passive voice is useful when the process, result, or unknown actor is the point.

Repeated participial tails often carry important reasoning vaguely: “..., highlighting..., underscoring..., demonstrating...” Decide whether the tail adds evidence, inference, or empty emphasis. Give a genuine inference its own clause and correct level of certainty. Delete empty emphasis. Do not ban participles.

Control overload by identifying the main assertion and its dependencies. Split at a meaningful relationship. Avoid turning one coherent sentence into four equally emphatic fragments.

When combining sentences, preserve their stated relationship. Adjacency alone does not license a new cause, reason, sequence, or implication. A fluent “so” can change the reasoning even when both clauses retain their original facts.

Retain normal repetition when it preserves precision: a technical component does not need three synonyms. Vary sentence openings only when repetition distracts or obscures emphasis. Do not replace correct language with rarer words merely to increase lexical diversity.

## 4. Tone and ownership

Identify what the writer is doing: reporting, objecting, thanking, requesting, explaining, selling, remembering, or speculating. The prose should perform that act without a layer of assistant performance around it.

- Remove generic praise and reassurance when they evade the substantive issue.
- Keep a justified negative judgment; do not replace “the screen is unreadable outdoors” with “there is room for an enhanced viewing experience.”
- Retain genuine uncertainty. Remove “perhaps” only if it is verbal padding, not an epistemic condition.
- Do not manufacture “I think,” personal stories, fatigue, vulnerability, humour, or sensory detail as human signals.
- Use contractions, colloquialisms, and occasional fragments where the author's usage and context support them. They are choices of register, not universal improvements.
- Keep roughness that communicates character; fix roughness that obstructs understanding. For an explicitly requested formal proofread, correct spelling and grammar rather than preserving errors as identity.

Style imitation should concern observable language choices. Never import a reference author's biographical claims, opinions, company details, or private experiences into the user's text.

## 5. Vocabulary and punctuation

Words such as “delve,” “robust,” “pivotal,” “landscape,” “tapestry,” and “leverage” deserve inspection when vague or overused. They remain ordinary words with legitimate meanings. “Robust standard errors” must not become “strong standard mistakes.” A landscape architect may need “landscape.”

Repeated em dashes, symmetrical triads, rhetorical questions, bold labels, and “from X to Y” constructions can feel automatic when they do no work. Repair the function, not just the symbol. Keep a dash that marks an interruption, a triad that enumerates three real things, or a question the text actually investigates.

Do not create numerical targets for perplexity, sentence-length variance, rare words, emotional intensity, passive-voice frequency, or edit distance. A detector's learned association is not a prescription for this writer.

## 6. Fidelity checks that catch quiet drift

Read the revised text as a set of assertions. Then compare each assertion with the source. Pay particular attention to:

| Original distinction | Dangerous rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| “May improve” | “Improves” |
| “No evidence of an effect” | “Evidence of no effect” |
| “Not evidence of equivalence” | “Does not prove equivalence” |
| “Associated with” | “Caused” |
| “Proposed / planned / simulated” | “Implemented / observed / verified” |
| “Up to 20% in this sample” | “20% for users” |
| “Reduced **by** 20%” | “Reduced **to** 20%” |
| “Must” / “may” / “should” | A different obligation |
| “Only if A” | “If A” |
| “Some participants” | “Participants generally” |
| “The author argues...” | An unattributed fact |

Also check quote boundaries, references attached to the right claim, units, dates, denominator changes, ordinal direction, and who promises to do what. Preserve both the proposition and its epistemic status: “this is not evidence of equivalence” rules out an evidentiary interpretation; “this does not prove equivalence” can still allow supportive evidence. An embedding score, word diff, or clean script report cannot replace this comparison.

## 7. Work with incomplete or flawed source material

If a paragraph says little, a faithful humanized version may still say little. Choose the useful action within the brief:

- Remove redundant material and make the limited claim clear.
- In a review, identify the missing fact with a precise question.
- In a draft, use an explicit placeholder only if placeholders are acceptable.
- Offer substantive additions separately when they would need new evidence or decisions.

When two source claims conflict, preserve and flag the conflict rather than arbitrarily resolving it. When the source contains a known factual error, distinguish correction from stylistic editing. Do not make a false statement more persuasive through polished certainty.

## 8. Stop when the text is doing its job

After the initial rewrite, repair actual residual problems and run the source comparison. Retain a strong passage unchanged. If a new version is merely different, prefer the one closer to the writer's voice and intended meaning. “More edited” is not a quality measure.
