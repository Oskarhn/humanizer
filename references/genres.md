# Genre-specific decisions

Read the section relevant to the task. Natural writing depends on the situation; the same transformation can help an email and damage a methods section.

## Academic and technical prose

Make the argument and method easier to follow while preserving disciplinary precision. Formality is not an error.

- Keep the research question, population, intervention or exposure, comparator, outcome, evidence type, uncertainty, and limitations.
- Retain legitimate terms such as “robust regression,” “statistically significant,” and “nominalization.” Do not make terminology less accurate because it appears on an AI-word list.
- Use passive constructions when the procedure or unknown actor is the point. Do not insert an unsupported “we.”
- Preserve citation identifiers and their supporting claims. Do not move a citation so that it appears to support an added inference. Leave direct quotations exact unless correction is explicitly authorized and marked.
- Preserve equations, signs, subscripts, code, sample sizes, intervals, and units. Explain them in surrounding prose if requested; do not rewrite their contents as style.
- “Not statistically significant” does not mean “no effect.” “Consistent with” does not mean “proves.” An observational association remains an association.
- Keep necessary headings and conclusions. An abstract often needs an explicit result and limitation even when a social post would sound repetitive.
- In edit-only work, do not add new literature or silently repair references from memory. Flag a questionable citation for a separate verification step.

In engineering reports, distinguish design calculations, simulation, bench measurements, field observations, and certification. Preserve whether work is planned, executed, or verified, and by what method. Improved prose must not turn a simulated limit into physical evidence.

## Business reports, proposals, and decisions

Identify the decision, reasons, costs, alternatives, and next action. Remove aspirational language that obscures who will do what.

- Retain the actual decision-maker, budget, time horizon, success criterion, and unresolved dependency.
- Replace “drive value” with the specific benefit already supported in the source. If no benefit is supplied, flag the gap instead of inventing savings or testimonials.
- Preserve distinctions among a recommendation, a commitment, an option, and a guarantee.
- Do not erase an unattractive trade-off to make the proposal sound complete.
- Keep tables and lists when they make choices easier to compare. Do not turn a useful decision brief into an essay to avoid an imagined AI signature.

## Emails, letters, announcements, and personal messages

Preserve the real relationship between sender and recipient. State the request or relevant event with the appropriate amount of context.

- Keep action, owner, deadline, and requested response clear.
- Retain greetings and courtesy that fit the relationship. Remove a long ceremonial lead-in if it delays a simple request.
- Do not invent affection, regret, urgency, enthusiasm, or personal history.
- Preserve legitimate frustration when the user wants it expressed. Make the objection intelligible without laundering it into praise.
- For sensitive messages, prefer the writer's actual meaning over a stock empathetic script. Do not add promises to resolve an issue unless the source authorizes them.

## Journalism, explainers, and public information

Keep observed facts separate from claims, interpretation, and prediction. Make attribution clear. Do not replace a source's allegation with an unqualified assertion.

Explain necessary context and mechanisms using verified material. Concrete examples help only when true or explicitly illustrative. Do not invent a named person, local scene, statistic, or reported quote. A neutral tone need not make evidence and speculation sound equally credible.

## Marketing and product copy

Natural copy can still be persuasive. Anchor persuasion in actual product behavior, relevant situations, and supported advantages. Preserve eligibility, pricing qualifiers, exclusions, and limitations.

Avoid generic superlatives and artificial excitement where a concrete benefit would be clearer. Do not replace them with invented customer stories, fabricated urgency, exaggerated outcomes, or an unsupported first-person review. Do not remove required disclosures as “AI-sounding boilerplate.”

## Fiction and creative nonfiction

Separate **line editing** from **creative development**. Humanizing a story is not automatic permission to change plot, chronology, character motives, relationships, or its ending. Creative nonfiction retains factual obligations even when its language is literary.

For line editing:

- Preserve point of view, narrative distance, tense, scene logic, and established character voice.
- Inspect explanation that tells the reader what a scene already demonstrates. Cut a repeated moral when the action carries it; keep reflective narration when that is the chosen voice.
- Replace a cliché with a more fitting expression, not a different cliché. Concrete detail must come from the manuscript unless the user permits invention.
- Let dialogue perform the characters' actions and relationships. Do not make every character articulate the theme in the same fluent voice.
- Preserve productive ambiguity. Do not resolve an uncertain motive through narrator explanation.

For an authorized developmental rewrite, examine agency, conflicting goals, consequences, event escalation, subplot function, and how information is revealed. Offer changes because they strengthen this story. Do not add flashbacks, moral ambiguity, loose ends, or bleakness solely because they correlated with human writing in StoryScope [R07]. The findings concern a particular corpus, not universal rules of good fiction.

A tightly linear plot, happy ending, or explicit theme can be intentional and effective. Do not enforce a reverse template in the name of originality.

## Documents with formal or protected language

When a document includes contracts, legal definitions, safety instructions, consent language, disclosures, or regulated claims, preserve their operative meaning and exact wording when required. Do not casually replace “shall,” “may,” “only,” “unless,” or defined terms. Where simplification would require a substantive decision, supply it separately and identify the effect. Do not add a generic legal or safety warning to ordinary editing work.
